# Professional register notes

## Observations

- In the examined PRs, issues, and mailing-list discussions, the problem, proposal, or objection appears in the opening one or two sentences. This supports point-first defaults for those artifacts, not a universal rule for every professional document. [S1, S2, S7, S8, S11]
- Review disagreement is commonly tied to a concrete technical consequence, maintenance cost, or alternative. Questions are useful when the reviewer is genuinely uncertain. [S1, S6, S8, S11]
- Epistemic qualifiers such as `I think`, `my understanding is`, and `I believe` sit near the proposition they limit. They should not be deleted when uncertainty is real. [S2, S5, S7, S11]
- Contractions appear naturally in conversational professional artifacts and practitioner essays. Uncontracted forms remain appropriate in specifications, contracts, deliberate emphasis, and more formal audiences.
- Functional fragments and workflow shorthand occur in reviews and coordination, but dramatic one-word staccato should not be injected into professional prose without voice evidence.
- Exact code identifiers, citations, units, issue links, and first-use acronym mappings remain legitimate. The editing target is nested clutter or repeated hedge stacking, not parentheses themselves.

## Useful inherited material

The old package remains substantive input for both new skills:

- meaning, modality, attribution, and instruction/context separation;
- formal-tone, transition-inflation, balanced-hedging, parenthesis, and convoluted-phrasing review cues;
- functional rhythm editing and faithful constructed fixtures;
- explicit guards against invented facts and borrowed biography.

Its archived counts, zero-occurrence claims, source dates, equivalence tables, and rhythm coefficients remain inherited or unvalidated unless separately rechecked. They do not become professional-writing statistics.

## Operational implications

- Front the point in routine email, PR, issue, review, update, and chat artifacts unless courtesy or context genuinely needs to come first.
- Prefer active agents and concrete verbs where they preserve responsibility and precision.
- Keep uncertainty adjacent to its claim.
- Use concrete options for actionable trade-offs; retain a balanced comparison when the requested artifact genuinely requires one.
- Avoid ceremonial openings, transition stacks, bureaucratic nominalizations, and canned summary closers when they add no function.
- Do not manufacture slang, typos, lowercase styling, or theatrical fragments to make professional prose feel human.
